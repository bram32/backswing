Free Relief Monetization Brief

What this app can realistically earn, what it should charge, when it should start charging, and the good reasons it might never make money at all.

Written 6 September 2026 for a solo developer, pre-launch, iOS TestFlight and web. Competitor prices and ratings observed the same day.

The call, in one box

  1. Launch free, no paywall, no ads. At plausible year one volumes a paywall earns low four figures and costs the ratings velocity, word of mouth and clinician referrals that are your only distribution.
  2. The niche is open, and that is the real finding. The App Store returns one relevant app for "golf back pain", and everything positioned on pain rather than yards has under 200 ratings.
  3. Spend 90 days buying data, not revenue, using App Store Connect analytics, which need no SDK and do not change the "Data Not Collected" label.
  4. One gate: ship a paid tier only if day 28 retention clears 8 percent and installs clear 2,000 by day 90.
  5. Then sell a one time unlock: 29.99 USD, 24.99 GBP, 29.99 EUR. Nobody in this niche sells lifetime, and the loudest complaint about every competitor is the annual auto-renewal.

01What is actually being sold

Free Relief is three things stacked on each other: an unusual 3D swing lab with 24 articulated vertebrae and a load model, a large and careful body of general exercise content, and a privacy posture almost nobody in health and fitness can match, because there is no server.

Two of the three are commodity. The same movements are free on YouTube from people with faces and credentials, where Bob and Brad have 5.48 million subscribers and the largest golf fitness channel, TPI, has 103,000. Privacy is a beautiful differentiator that few consumers pay for. Only the 3D lab is hard to copy, and it has the weakest connection to a wallet. Nobody has ever paid a subscription for a diagram.

The uncomfortable finding

The best available meta-analysis of golf back pain, Smith et al. 2018, pooled 19 studies and found the evidence "does not support a relationship between swing characteristics and the development of golf-related pain." What does predict it is prior back pain, age and body mass. The swing lab is a superb explanatory device whose causal story runs ahead of the science. Say "here is where the load goes" and "here is how to build capacity", never "fix your swing to fix your back". Carrying that honestly on the evidence page will make the app more credible, not less.

02The market, honestly sized

The top line looks wonderful: 29.1 million US on-course golfers in 2025, the highest in nearly two decades (NGF), and another 43.9 million outside the US (R&A 2026).

The demographic fit is the strongest structural argument for the product. The average England Golf club member is 54.99 years old (England Golf). The WHO puts peak low back pain prevalence at ages 50 to 55. And among 1,170 male golfers with a mean age of 54.98, 37.3 percent had low back pain in the previous seven days, more than double the next body region, with no difference by handicap (Murray et al. 2023). Three independent datasets land on the same person.

Then the funnel narrows. NGF's core golfer, playing eight or more rounds a year, is about 13 million people in the US and accounts for roughly 90 percent of all golf spending. Call the serviceable market 20 million core English-speaking golfers, of whom 6 to 7 million have recurrent low back pain. Reaching 0.05 percent of that would be a good year.

Estimate, wide range

Year one installs for an English-only, iOS, unfunded health app in a narrow niche, with competent ASO plus one video that travels: low 800, base 3,000, high 12,000. Apple took 557,000 new app submissions in 2025, and 80 percent of apps that clear 100 downloads a month still get under 10,000 (Appfigures). Plan on the base case.

The apps charging 199 dollars are selling a person, not software. Justin Rose is the product at GolfForever, Mike Carroll at Fit For Golf. Free Relief has no face on it, by a defensible earlier decision to build credibility through an evidence layer rather than a clinician partnership. Good for honesty, expensive for pricing power.

The pain position is vacant. Searching for "golf back pain" returns one relevant app, then GPS apps and arcade games. "Golf injury prevention" returns 25 results and not one app positioned on injury prevention. "Golf mobility" returns apps matching the word "mobile". The irony is load bearing: GolfForever's legal entity is Back Story LLC and it sells pain relief routines, yet it markets on ten extra yards. The category leader has abandoned the position this app is built for.

Nobody sells a lifetime unlock. Across GolfForever, Fit For Golf, Golfshot, 18Birdies, Arccos, TheStack, Pliability, Fitbod and Sworkit, every one is recurring only.

The store rating hides the anger. Pliability holds 4.81 on nearly 10,000 App Store ratings and 1.4 on Trustpilot; Arccos holds 4.59 and 1.6. The grievance in both is the annual auto-conversion after a seven day trial, a surprise charge of 180 to 400 dollars, not the price. Sworkit, with a 14 day money-back guarantee, has almost no billing complaints. A one time price here is a weapon aimed at the loudest complaint in the category.

04The arithmetic

RevenueCat's 2026 dataset covers 115,000 apps. 17.3 percent of newly launched subscription apps ever reach 1,000 dollars a month and 4.6 percent reach 10,000; median monthly revenue one year after launch is about 72 dollars. Health and fitness realised revenue is 0.66 dollars per install at day 60, and the category refund rate is 4.71 percent. Apple takes 15 percent under the Small Business Program, which any new developer qualifies for. Enrol on day one; it is a form, not a negotiation.

The strongest argument against this brief

It is the hard paywall row. RevenueCat's 2026 data shows hard paywalls converting at 10.7 percent of downloads versus 2.1 percent for freemium, an eight times difference in revenue per install, for identical one year retention of 27 versus 28 percent. On those numbers the freemium tax is paid entirely up front, and the case for gating on day one is strong.

Three things push back, and on balance they win. That median includes apps with brands and review counts, so a new app with zero ratings behind a hard paywall converts nowhere near it. Ratings and downloads feed App Store search ranking, so gating suppresses the exact signal ASO needs in the first 90 days. And a hard paywall closes the physiotherapist and club professional route, the one channel where "free, no account, nothing leaves your phone" is an advantage to a gatekeeper. Revisit this at five figures of installs. At 3,000, the gap between the two models is about 6,000 dollars, which is not worth the distribution it costs.

05The regulatory floor

App Review is the first real risk, and it is not about money. The iOS app renders in a web view, and guideline 4.2 requires an app to "include features, content, and UI that elevate it beyond a repackaged website" (App Review Guidelines). The mitigations are in place and they are good: everything is bundled and served from a local custom scheme, three.js is vendored locally, and it renders a real time WebGL scene with interactive hit testing. Put that in the review notes rather than hoping. One loose end remains. The web fonts still load from Google's CDN, so "nothing leaves your device" is not strictly true on the web build, because the browser hands the user's IP address to a third party to fetch a font. Self host them.

Guideline 1.4.1 gives extra scrutiny to medical apps "that could provide inaccurate data or information" and says apps "should remind users to check with a doctor". The app does this on every plan. Never let a paywall sit between a user and a red flag warning.

Medical device status. Software sits outside the US device definition when it is "for maintaining or encouraging a healthy lifestyle and is unrelated to the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, prevention, or treatment of a disease or condition" (FDA); MDR Rule 11 and MDCG 2019-11 apply the same logic in Europe. The mobility self-screen walks at that line: measuring a user and outputting a personalised program resembles individualised recommendation, and a paywall reframes it from education to product claim. Keep outputs as ranges and general advice.

Privacy is an asset one SDK away from being a liability. With no server, accounts or analytics the nutrition label is "Data Not Collected" and the GDPR surface is nil. Add analytics or crash reporting and you become a controller needing a lawful basis, health-adjacent inputs become special category data, and the label changes.

Claims. The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated in July 2025, but ROSCA, state auto-renewal laws and the UK ASA still bite. Never write cure, treat, clinically proven, or prevents injury.

06Nine ways to make money

LaterFreemium with a Pro unlock. The only option with real upside, and the boundary matters more than the price. Behind it: multi-week programs, the personalised avatar, the tempo trainer. In front of it permanently: the planner, the red flags, all 45 exercises, the routines, the swing lab, the log and the evidence page. Charging for the citations would undo the point of having them.

PreferredOne time unlock rather than subscription. Lower lifetime value per payer, higher conversion, no churn, no cancellation rage, no negative-option exposure, and differentiated in a category where nobody offers it. It fits an app with no server costs and a user whose success means he stops needing you. The objection that lifetime pricing caps revenue is true and irrelevant at 3,000 installs.

NoAdvertising. Three reasons, any one sufficient. It destroys the "Data Not Collected" label, at base case volumes it earns a sandwich a month, and a supplement ad next to back pain guidance is exactly what makes health apps untrustworthy.

NoAffiliate links. Amazon Associates pays around 1 percent in Sports and in Health and Personal Care, so a 40 dollar massage ball earns 40 cents and 100 dollars a month means selling 250 of them. A recommendation engine inside a pain app also carries an obvious conflict.

SmallTip jar. Guideline 3.1.1 permits in-app purchase "to enable customers to 'tip' the developer", so on iOS Apple takes its cut, while on the web a Stripe or Ko-fi link is unencumbered. Expect well under one percent to tip. Worth an afternoon, because it converts goodwill into a willingness-to-pay signal you cannot otherwise get.

One pilotClubs, coaches and physios. The seductive option and the biggest time sink. Do one pilot with one local club for the story and the testimonials, and build no white-label anything. The real asset is not revenue: a physiotherapist can recommend a free, account-free app without a data protection conversation.

Not yetCorporate wellness. Hinge Health's published price is 995 dollars per participant per year, it will not sell to an individual at any price, and with clinicians and a sales force behind it still converts only 3.9 percent of 20 million eligible lives. No free golf app enters this market.

OpportunisticSponsorship. One tasteful credit from a golf physio clinic or a mobility brand, possible only once an audience exists, and arriving through content rather than the app.

NowCost control as strategy. Nothing beats a zero run rate: no server and no accounts means no bill, no password reset inbox and no breach. RevenueCat is free below 2,500 dollars of monthly tracked revenue, and StoreKit 2 alone handles one non-consumable. Do not add a payments platform before there are payments.

07Eight points of view

The 52 year old with a sore back

"I do not want a subscription. I want to know if I should play on Saturday." He loves the 3D spine for ninety seconds, then judges the app on whether the first-tee routine helps.

Worries: another thing to remember. Retention lives or dies on the seven minute warm-up, not the anatomy.

The physiotherapist

"Better content than most, and the model is a simplification, so say so." She approves of the red flags and the refusal to diagnose, and hands out a free app before a paid one.

Worries: the 13 degrees figure read as a hard threshold, and someone with radicular pain rotating because an app suggested it.

The golf coach

"I can use this to show him why I keep saying turn your ribs." The lab is a teaching aid, and coaches are the most likely evangelists available.

Worries: that it replaces him. Frame it as explaining what he already says, never as a substitute for a lesson.

The App Store reviewer

"Is this a repackaged website?" He has four minutes. He opens it, sees a spinning skeleton, and either is impressed or files a 4.2.

Worries: health claims and web wrappers. Lead the review notes with airplane mode and the local scheme.

The angel investor

"Lovely craft. Where is the wedge?" He sees no distribution, no data moat, no recurring revenue and a founder who ruled out the obvious credibility shortcuts.

Worries: that this is a portfolio piece with a business plan taped to it. He is not wrong, and that is fine if venture scale was never the point.

The skeptic

"It fails because nobody finds it." Not because it is bad. "Free Relief" has no search volume, the live domain still says backswing, there is no face and no list, and the north is heading into winter.

Worries: that six more features get built instead of one video getting made.

The ethicist

"You are selling to people in pain, so the honesty bar is higher." Exercise for chronic low back pain has effects that are real but pooled across comparisons small and not clinically important, and there is still no completed trial showing a golf warm-up prevents injury.

Worries: a paywall between a person in pain and the line telling them to see a doctor. Never build that.

The developer's own time

Every hour on StoreKit, receipts, restores and refunds is an hour not spent on the one video that would bring users. Payments are a week minimum, and worth about 1,500 dollars in the base case.

Worries: burning the fun. Free apps do not generate billing support email.

08The recommendation

Launch completely free, with no paywall, no ads and no account, and say so loudly, because "free, no account, nothing leaves your phone" is differentiated, true, and aimed at the loudest complaint about every competitor in the table. Then use 90 days to find out whether anyone comes back on day 28.

If the gate clears, ship Free Relief Pro as a one time unlock containing the multi-week programs and the personalised avatar, and add a tip jar with it. Keep every safety and evidence surface free forever. Note the naming friction now: an app called Free Relief that charges for anything invites a joke, so the free tier must stay substantial enough that the name stays honest.

10The first 90 days, in time rather than money

  1. Own the golf and back-care keyword intersection, using the name, subtitle and keyword field in the listing document. "Golf" is the moat: it disqualifies Bend (173,220 ratings) and Hinge Health (162,310), who will never want it. Four hours, and it compounds.
  2. Rebuild the first three screenshots around the lumbar load visualisation. Apple says "the first one to three images will appear in search results when no app preview is available", and StoreMaven's 500 million session dataset finds 60 percent never scroll past. This multiplies action one rather than adding to it.
  3. Make one 30 second silent captioned video of the lab. Play the swing, toggle stiff hips, watch the lumbar bar go red. Reuse it as the app preview, the nomination supplement and the social asset. Median outcome is a few hundred views; the tail is the only genuine upside here.
  4. Submit a Featuring Nomination in App Store Connect, type App Launch, three weeks ahead at minimum. It takes five supplemental URLs including a TestFlight link, and Apple's stated criteria include innovation and uniqueness, which is what the lab is.
  5. Participate, do not post, in r/backpain (83k) and r/golftips (96k), the two best matched communities. r/GolfSwing bans self-promotion, r/physicaltherapy is gated to licensed clinicians, and GolfWRX requires prior approval, so three of the most obvious audiences are structurally closed.
  6. Write six articles on the web version targeting golf lower back pain, reverse spine angle, first tee warm-up, S posture, golfer's elbow and thoracic rotation. The only channel you own outright.
  7. Email 20 club professionals and 10 physiotherapists with one paragraph, one image and a printable QR poster. Untested and cheap to test, and the first credible reviews matter more than the installs.
  8. Pitch MyGolfSpy's "We Tried It" format, and only that. Golf Monthly and Today's Golfer run roundups of major brands only, with no submission route.

11What not to do

  • Do not build accounts or cloud sync. It destroys the privacy claim, creates GDPR obligations, adds cost, and nobody asked for it.
  • Do not add ads, ever.
  • Do not paywall safety content, red flags or the evidence page.
  • Do not fight for "back pain", "sciatica" or "posture" as head terms. Bend and Hinge Health have over 170,000 and 160,000 ratings, and chasing them dilutes the keyword field.
  • Do not launch on Product Hunt. Non-featured launches get 100 to 500 visitors and 1 to 15 signups, only a tenth get featured, and 89 percent of surveyed founders would not do it again. The audience is founders, not 55 year olds with sore backs.
  • Do not keyword-stuff the description. Apple indexes name, subtitle, keywords and category; description and promotional text do not affect search ranking, by Apple's own statement.
  • Do not buy installs. Apple Search Ads in Health and Fitness run about 3.02 dollars per download against a freemium revenue per install of 0.38, roughly eight times underwater.
  • Do not ship Android, translations, or a second video before day 90.
  • Do not claim clinical outcomes or imply a clinician endorses this unless one does and is named.
  • Do not build six more features before making the video. The backlog is not the bottleneck. Distribution is.
